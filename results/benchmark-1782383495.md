#### Prompt

```text
A disease affects 1% of people. A test is 99% sensitive and has a 5% false-positive rate. If a person tests positive, approximately what is the probability they have the disease?
```

#### Correct Answer

```text
Approximately 16.7%. By Bayes' rule, 0.99*0.01 / (0.99*0.01 + 0.05*0.99) = 0.0099/0.0594 = 1/6.
```

#### Response

```text
Approximately **16.7%** (or exactly **1/6**).

**Why?** This is a classic application of Bayes' theorem. You can see it clearly with a hypothetical population:

- Imagine **10,000** people.
- **1%** have the disease → **100** sick, **9,900** healthy.
- The test catches **99%** of the sick → **99 true positives**.
- The test incorrectly flags **5%** of the healthy → `0.05 × 9,900 = 495 false positives`.
- Total positive tests = `99 + 495 = 594`.

If you test positive, your chance of actually having the disease is:
`99 / 594 = 1/6 ≈ 16.7%`

**Key insight:** Even though the test seems highly accurate, the disease is rare. The low base rate means the small false-positive rate generates far more false alarms than true cases. This counterintuitive result is a well-known example of the **base rate fallacy** in probability and statistics.
```

At 3:15, the minute hand points exactly at the 3, which is **90°** from the 12 o'clock position. 

The hour hand, however, moves continuously. At exactly 3:00 it's at 90°, but it advances **0.5° per minute**. In 15 minutes, it moves 15 × 0.5° = **7.5°** past the 3. So the hour hand is at 90° + 7.5° = **97.5°**.

The difference between the hands is 97.5° − 90° = **7.5°**.

The smaller angle is **7.5 degrees**.
